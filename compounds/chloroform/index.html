Molar mass of chloroform (CHCl3)

The molar mass of chloroform, CHCl3, is 119.37 g/mol. One mole of it, which is 6.022 × 1023 molecules, has a mass of 119.37 grams.

119.37 g/mol

12.011 + 1.0080 + 3 × 35.45 = 119.369 · 5 atoms in the formula · also called trichloromethane

Open in the calculator

About chloroform

Chloroform is a heavy, sweet-smelling liquid once used as an anaesthetic and now as a solvent. Three chlorine atoms give almost nine tenths of its mass.

Other names: trichloromethane.

The working

The formula CHCl3 has 1 carbon atom, 1 hydrogen atom and 3 chlorine atoms, 5 atoms in all. Multiply each count by the atomic weight of the element and add:

12.011 + 1.0080 + 3 × 35.45 = 119.369 g/mol

With the rounded atomic masses used in many school books (C = 12, H = 1, Cl = 35.5) the answer is 119.5 g/mol. The difference from 119.37 is too small to matter in most school sums.

Percentage composition

By mass, CHCl3 is 10.06% carbon, 0.84% hydrogen and 89.09% chlorine. The largest share comes from chlorine. In a 100 g sample there are 10.1 g of carbon, 0.8 g of hydrogen and 89.1 g of chlorine.

Worked examples

Grams to moles. How many moles are in 40 g of CHCl3? Divide the mass by the molar mass: 40 ÷ 119.37 = 0.3351 mol.

Moles to grams. What is the mass of 3 mol of CHCl3? Multiply: 3 × 119.37 = 358.1 g.

Counting particles. 40 g contains 0.3351 mol × 6.022 × 1023 = 2.018 × 1023 molecules, and 1.009 × 1024 atoms in all.

Questions people ask

What is the molar mass of CHCl3?

The molar mass of chloroform (CHCl3) is 119.37 g/mol, or 119.369 g/mol before rounding, using IUPAC abridged atomic weights.

How many moles are in 100 g of CHCl3?

100 ÷ 119.37 = 0.8377 mol.

What is the mass of one molecule of CHCl3?

Divide the molar mass by the Avogadro constant: 119.37 ÷ 6.022 × 1023 = 1.982 × 10-22 g.

What is the percentage of chlorine in CHCl3?

Chlorine makes up 89.09% of the mass of CHCl3: 3 × 35.45 ÷ 119.369 × 100.

Which values? This site uses the abridged standard atomic weights published by IUPAC (CIAAW), read on 12 November 2026. Your textbook or question paper may round them differently, for example H = 1 and O = 16, so its answer can differ slightly. In an exam, use the values the paper gives.
